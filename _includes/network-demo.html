Academics meet at conferences. At least theoretically, they play a game: based on a simple cost-benefit decision, they decide whether to write a paper or not with the people they meet. I.e., they form and cut links to form a network. Each academic values every other academic they can reach, discounted by the correlation between their interests and paying a cost for exerting effort in the paper they write (illustrated by the link):

\[ u_i(g) \;=\; \sum_{j \neq i} \delta^{d(i,j)} \;-\; c \, d_i(g), \]

where \(d(i,j)\) is the length of the shortest path between \(i\) and \(j\) and \(d_i(g)\) is the number of links of \(i\). At every step one pair revises its link if it is profitable to do so, until the network is pairwise stable. Move the sliders to change the incentives; hover over a player to see their payoff, or drag them around.

Start from:

Links: Total welfare: Efficient welfare:

Cheap links: \(c < \delta - \delta^2\) (here \(\delta - \delta^2 =\) ). The complete network is efficient and is the unique pairwise stable network.

Intermediate cost: \(\delta - \delta^2 < c < \delta\) (here \(\delta - \delta^2 =\) ). A star is efficient; a star is pairwise stable, but other stable networks can arise too.

Stability and efficiency diverge: \(\delta < c < \delta + \tfrac{(n-2)\,\delta^2}{2}\) (here the upper bound is ). Nobody wants to start a link from scratch, so the empty network is stable, yet a star would be efficient.

Expensive links: \(c > \delta + \tfrac{(n-2)\,\delta^2}{2}\) (here ). The empty network is efficient.
